# How to connect three tarot cards

To turn three separate meanings into an answer people can discuss, focus on the relationships between the cards.

If you draw the Eight of Pentacles, Four of Cups and Three of Pentacles and say only “effort, boredom, cooperation”, you have not yet answered the question. Keywords are like three tools laid on a table: a reading still needs to explain which is operating, which presents an obstacle, and which might change the situation.

The following original exercise uses RWS imagery to demonstrate that process. It is a method of reflection and storytelling, without guaranteed predictions, and cannot replace checking real-world evidence.

## Decide what the three cards are answering first

Imagine a designer asking, “I keep revising my portfolio. How can I make my next round of job preparation more effective?” Before turning the cards, she assigns three positions: what I am putting effort into, where I may get stuck, and what I can try next.

This sets limits on interpretation. The Four of Cups in an “obstacle” position might raise questions about attention and engagement; in an “available resource” position it might invite discussion of careful selection or delaying acceptance. A position changes the angle of the question, but does not allow any card to mean anything at will.

Nor should “situation, obstacle, advice” suddenly become “past, present, future” after you see the cards. That makes the interpretation easier to justify to itself and harder to examine. Write down the question and positions before practising, and keep them as written.

## Describe the image before interpreting it

First, the Eight of Pentacles: a craftsperson is making pentacles, with completed pieces neatly displayed. Waite's text associates it with work, skill and preparation. For now, note “continuing to refine a specific piece of work”. [Waite: Eight of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe08.htm)

Second, the Four of Cups: a seated figure has three cups in front, and another is being offered, but he does not reach to receive it. Note “something is offered from outside; a response has not yet occurred”. “Boredom” is already an interpretation; not taking the cup is the visual evidence we can discuss. [Waite: Four of Cups](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktcu04.htm)

Third, the Three of Pentacles: a craftsperson works within a building, with two other people nearby, one holding a plan. Waite emphasises skilled work and explicitly asks the reader to compare it with the Eight of Pentacles. “Bring the work into a shared discussion” develops a reading from the picture; it is not a word-for-word definition in his text. [Waite: Three of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe03.htm)

This distinction matters: separating “I can see” from “I infer” lets a reader identify which steps are well supported and which need more information.

## Add a connecting word between neighbouring cards

Modern tarot teaching includes a technique called bridging: making visual or intuitive connections between symbols within the question's context. It is an interpretive exercise, not a validated mechanism of prediction. [Barbara Moore: Practical Techniques for Better Readings](https://www.llewellyn.com/blog/2019/09/practical-techniques-for-better-readings/)

In this example, look first only at the Eight of Pentacles and Four of Cups. One person keeps working; the other does not receive what is offered. Place “but” between them: “You keep refining the work, but may have become reluctant to take in fresh feedback.”

Then compare the Four of Cups and Three of Pentacles. In one picture a person keeps a distance from what is offered; in the other, several people gather around work. Connect them with “by”: “By inviting others into the discussion, examine whether you are rejecting unhelpful advice or any advice that would change the existing work.”

Finally combine this into one answer: “Pause a round of solitary polishing, discuss the work with someone who can explain the standards it needs to meet, then decide what to change next.” The advice has a visual basis, but “you are rejecting feedback” remains a hypothesis to check.

## Use counterexamples to keep the story open to correction

If the designer replies, “I ask three people to look every week; their advice just conflicts,” the interpretation needs to change. You cannot insist that she unconsciously rejects feedback anyway, making the cards right whatever she says.

A version that fits the information better is: repeated revisions are draining you; too much advice is difficult to absorb; next you need a shared evaluation standard rather than more reviewers. The plan in the Three of Pentacles can now serve as a visual prompt for grounding discussion in shared requirements. A pictured plan does not inherently mean hiring criteria. The connection comes from this question, and we should say so.

Only comparison with the target role, the actual work and previous feedback can show which version is more useful. If real information supports neither, acknowledge that the association has not offered anything useful this time.

## Turn one interpretation into one action

Set a small experiment for this exercise: choose one target role and someone familiar with it. Ask them to look at just one portfolio project and identify the decision process they find hardest to understand. Record the feedback, then make one clearly bounded revision.

A week later, check whether the changes were more focused and whether the other person can explain more quickly what problem the project solves. These are observable changes. They cannot prove the cards foresaw the future, but they can help you judge whether the reading led to clear action.

## Try it yourself

Choose any three cards, first assigning positions for a specific, low-risk question. Write one non-judgmental description of each image, then connect adjacent cards with “but”, “therefore” or “by”. Once you have a one-sentence answer, write an alternative interpretation that fits the images equally well and say what real information would make you choose it instead.

Finish with just one action the person can take. If the answer still amounts to “the universe will arrange it” or “they will definitely come back”, return to the question, positions and images and connect them again. A coherent reading allows others to follow your reasoning and challenge its assumptions.
