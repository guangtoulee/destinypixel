# Why the four Fours read differently

All are Fours, yet one raises flowers, one folds their arms, one lies still and one grips tightly. The differences lie in how the figures relate to the world around them.

Lay the RWS Four of Wands, Four of Cups, Four of Swords and Four of Pentacles side by side, without opening a book of meanings. Ask just three questions: Who can move? Where are the objects? Is there a way between the person and the outside world? This group is a useful exercise in testing abstract ideas against images.

Modern interpretations often treat “four” as stability, structure or boundaries. This is a framework of numerical symbolism, not a natural law shared by every deck. Even if we provisionally use that framework, we still need to ask: what has become stable, and at what cost? The comparison below centres on RWS imagery and offers one way of reading, rather than a single answer for all four cards.

## Start with hands and feet

In the Four of Wands, four staffs stand in the foreground with a garland strung between them, while figures behind raise bouquets. The staffs do not weigh down their bodies, and nobody needs to clutch them to keep the structure standing. Waite associates the card with peace, harmony and the repose of a home. [The Pictorial Key to the Tarot: Four of Wands](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktwa04.htm)

This supports a possible interpretation: an arrangement has become reliable enough for people to give their attention to shared life. In a team, it might prompt you to examine whether the division of work is functioning and whether a milestone deserves collective recognition. There is no marriage certificate in the picture, so declaring a wedding merely because you see a garland goes beyond what the image supports.

The Four of Pentacles offers a contrast: the figure holds one pentacle against the chest, presses one beneath each foot, and has another above the head. Waite's description emphasises holding on to what one has. [The Pictorial Key to the Tarot: Four of Pentacles](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktpe04.htm)

Stability here requires the body's continuous involvement. Try imagining that posture: with your arms and feet occupied, how much freedom remains to receive something new? This supports a reading in which protecting resources can reduce flexibility, but one card cannot establish that someone is miserly. Caution on a tight budget and unwillingness to cooperate despite having ample resources may look similar while arising from different circumstances.

## Two kinds of stillness

The figure in the Four of Cups sits under a tree, with three cups in front and another offered by a hand extending from a cloud. He does not reach to take it. Waite writes of dissatisfaction with one's surroundings and weariness after satisfaction. [The Pictorial Key to the Tarot: Four of Cups](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktcu04.htm)

The key question is not whether there are enough things, but whether something new can enter one's attention. For reflection, ask: do I really not need this option, or has fatigue temporarily drained my interest in everything? The two answers call for different next steps; “missing an opportunity” cannot decide between them in advance.

The Four of Swords places stillness in a different setting. Waite explicitly identifies the central figure as a knight's tomb effigy in a posture of prayer; it should not simply be treated as a living person asleep. His interpretations include retreat, solitude and rest. [The Pictorial Key to the Tarot: Four of Swords](https://sacred-texts.com/tarot/pkt/pktsw04.htm)

Modern readers often use this to discuss pausing, recovery or delaying a response. That is a practical application of a still image, not a diagnosis or prediction of death from a card. Compared with the Four of Cups, a useful question is: am I reducing stimulation so that I can think again, or am I shutting out every suggestion? A single card cannot identify your motive for you, but it can help make the question more specific.

## Four prompts for the same situation

Suppose the reading group you run with friends has reached its third month, and you are deciding whether to expand. This is an original exercise, not a record of an actual draw.

- Four of Wands: Keep the processes that already work, and let members share what they have achieved. Stability comes from arrangements everyone can use together.
- Four of Cups: Find out why engagement is falling first. Adding activities may not resolve fatigue; it may simply offer another cup.
- Four of Swords: Allow the organisers time to review and take a break from the schedule. A short pause can have clear limits and a return date.
- Four of Pentacles: Check who controls the budget and decision-making authority. Reserves have value, but one person holding every decision may also impede cooperation.

None of these cards is inherently “best”. What matters is whether stability at this stage gives people spare capacity or makes movement difficult.

## A new deck calls for new questions

When comparing decks, first inspect what the particular version actually shows. Early pip cards often indicate their number through repeated suit signs, without the human scenes found in RWS. The posture of refusal beneath a tree cannot simply be transferred to every Four of Cups. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art: tarot's history and structure](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/tarot-2)

Interpretive traditions also disagree. In his 1888 booklet, for example, Mathers lists the Four of Pentacles in terms of pleasure and contentment, unlike Waite's emphasis on possession. The modern editor of that text specifically cautions that this early work should not be equated with the Golden Dawn's later internal system of correspondences. [Mathers: The Tarot and the editorial note](https://hermeticgoldendawn.org/the-tarot/)

The value of comparison is therefore not finding an eternally correct dictionary. It is learning to explain which images and tradition you are using, and how you move from an image to an interpretation.

## A five-minute exercise

Choose the four Fours. Write one action word for each, then “what this action protects” and “what it restricts”. For the same real-life question, propose one practical action for each card. Finally remove any assertion for which you can identify neither a visual basis nor a way to check it in real life. Explaining where an interpretation comes from is more useful than memorising more keywords.
